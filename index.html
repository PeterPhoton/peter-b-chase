Peter B. Chase's poems have a fondness for life's physical and emotional flotsam: cardboard boxes, old sweaters, an elevator, a baby grand piano, a spinning wheel, a skull thrown from a balcony. Chase trusts the reader and almost never announces the significance of these objects, but lets the metaphorical weight accumulate as the highly personal verse unfolds.

His photographer's instinct — to put "little brackets" around things — is also his poet's instinct. The images are likewise highly eclectic — from exotic landscapes to surreal macro worlds to urban street photography. They are all made with the same restless instinct — to reveal what is evocative — whether the subject is a galaxy from a mountain top, or a naked mannequin in an alley.

Initially it all seems a bit of a dog's breakfast, but when you look a little deeper surprises surface: the stillness of a Shan shui painting, the unflinching directness of a Diane Arbus, or even, in his very best landscapes, the sweep of a Sebastião Salgado — all refracted through the vision of a guy who's been at it since the 1970s, and still hasn't decided what he wants to be when he grows up.

This melding of Light and Language — poetry and photography — does not come with a familiar formula. We cannot say the images illustrate the poetry or that the poetry is an explanation of the pictures. Seemingly random juxtapositions create unresolved tension that entices the viewer to envision the connection between the two — the results can be singular.

The gallery Dreams and Memories begins with this short but highly evocative poem.

Boxes
I carried cardboard boxes filled with books, pots and pans, and clothes,
up the stairs to my new apartment
I counted each of the twenty four steps
One for each year it had been, since I last lived alone

It's followed by a photograph of a sketch of a young boy, staring directly at the viewer. However it appears decayed and is falling out of its frame. The face is partially obscured by an adult hand that seems to be waving the viewer away, yet the direct gaze of the boy penetrates effortlessly. The result is unsettling but strangely poignant.

Mr. Chase calls it "Documentary Surrealism," which is either an authentic aesthetic philosophy or a very clever way of saying "I make it up as I go." Possibly both?

Mr. Chase studied art and photography properly, at actual universities, so there's concept and technique under the chaos. But his real education has been in the decades since, traveling the world second guessing his choice to carry a heavy pack with camera gear. He now lives in Virginia with his remarkable girlfriend and a zero-pet policy.

So is it art? Pity, but you'll have to decide. Peter's just going to keep making the pictures either way.

The Well — poem by Peter B Chase

Brilliantly bleached. Snickering two-inch canines, a mohawk of boar bristle. A find. We delighted and stashed him in my pack. Picking our way down the arroyo sweat rolling into squinting eyes stepping over forgotten twists of rusted barbed wire. The desiccated outpost just a tumble of adobe bricks a few flies buzzing around the crumbling well. You held my legs while I leaned over the edge the stone dropped two seconds before the miraculous tiny splash. I sketched that skull many decades on in a desultory still life or two. I posted you its image but you registered nothing. Not the days on the road, not the week in the desert. Some time later I was drunk and threw that skull off my balcony into the pond.

In Between — poem by Peter B Chase

It was in the old hotel. The high whitewashed walls and tall windows with the wet peeling sills. Iron rails clanking. Toy trains far below, horns unrelenting. Mist choking the hills. The small crusty loaf torn in half the crumbs scattered carelessly across the stained table. I heard the low rustling, the harried pacing, through the plaster walls. I stopped, listened, fascinated, embarrassed. The voice was slow rising, slowly pleading, slowly heaving. Bursting through he cried "Can't you just choose kindness first." A drop of rain traced a long slow path down the pane.

Pleading Quieting Thanking Breathing. It was more than a petition, it was less, begun when I was a fool, an equal among my peers. Dirty Hands — poem by Peter B Chase

Timmy lived in a house with an elevator, a baby grand piano and a spinning wheel. His family spent summers in Maine, sailing, and what else I have no idea. I have a picture somewhere of us, each planted in high chairs, in the kitchen. Timmy never took lessons but he would sometimes play the piano, head cocked, mouth slightly open. Timmy didn't go to my regular school, we didn't care or understand why. We built kingdoms under the shrubs and collected insects, stones and treasures with our dirty little hands. Timmy had a strict bedtime, I asked him once why he didn't protest, but he couldn't understand the question. The neighbor girl revealed her mysterious treasure to us behind the garage, of course we honored the pact and did the same. I slowly lost that invisible thread that bound us building the defensive kingdoms of boys and girls, embarrassment and competition, that Timmy couldn't understand.
